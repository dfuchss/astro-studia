The complaint is old and it is usually wrong. Latin is called a poor language
for philosophy, and the evidence offered is that it lacks the words. But a
language does not have words for something nobody has yet written about in it,
and acquiring them is the ordinary cost of going first.

## Three ways to do it

**Borrow.** Keep the Greek and let it naturalise. Cheap, and it works, but each
loan is a small debt the reader pays.

**Calque.** Build the word out of native parts in the same shape as the
original. `ποιότης` becomes _qualitas_. The reader can take it apart.

**Reach for something that exists.** The riskiest of the three, because a word
already in use arrives carrying everything it has meant before.

## What actually goes wrong

Not vocabulary. Vocabulary is solvable and mostly solved after one good book.

What goes wrong is _register_ — a distinction that was technical in the source
becoming ordinary in the target, so that a reader misses that a term is being
used precisely. You cannot fix that with a glossary. You fix it by writing
sentences that make the distinction do visible work.
